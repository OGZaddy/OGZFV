### Lime & Tomatoes — What’s the deal?

Think of lime (the **powdered rock**, not the margarita garnish) as a little soil‑spa treatment for your tomato beds. It’s mostly ground limestone—calcium carbonate (calcitic lime) or calcium + magnesium carbonate (dolomitic lime)—and it fixes two common tomato troubles:

| **Why you’d add lime** | **What it does** | **What can go wrong if you skip it** |
|------------------------|------------------|--------------------------------------|
| **Raises low pH** | Tomatoes are happiest around pH 6.2 – 6.8. Acidic soil (< 6.0) locks up nutrients. Lime nudges pH upward so roots can slurp up N‑P‑K, calcium, magnesium, etc. | Pale foliage, stunted growth, poor fruit set. |
| **Supplies calcium** | Calcium moves into fruit cell walls, keeping them firm. Adequate Ca²⁺ in the root zone (and even moisture) is the surest defense against **blossom‑end rot**. | Those ugly black, sunken bottoms that ruin otherwise perfect tomatoes. |

---

#### How to use it (no PhD required)

1. **Test first, spread later.**  
   A $10 soil test kit—or county extension test—tells you pH and Ca/Mg levels. Shoot for pH 6.2‑6.8.  
   *Blind‑liming is like salting soup before you taste it.*

2. **Pick your product.**  
   * **Calcitic (garden) lime** – mostly calcium. Great if your soil already has enough magnesium.  
   * **Dolomitic lime** – roughly 60 % CaCO₃, 40 % MgCO₃. Choose it if a soil test says you’re short on Mg as well as Ca.  
   * Skip **hydrated/quick lime** for veggies—it raises pH too fast and can burn roots.

3. **Work it in early.**  
   - **Rate**: 2–4 lb per 100 ft² on mildly acidic loam; sandy soils need less, heavy clay a bit more—follow test‐lab recs.  
   - **Timing**: Ideally 2–3 months before planting because it takes time to react (late fall or very early spring). For last‑minute beds, incorporate thoroughly 6–8 in deep and water well.

4. **Containers & raised beds.**  
   Potting mixes often come neutral, but peat‑heavy blends trend acidic over time. Mix in 1‑2 Tbsp dolomitic lime per gallon of mix at setup; repeat each season if tests show pH drifting down.

5. **Maintenance—not overkill.**  
   Re‑test every 2‑3 years. Too much lime pushes pH above 7 and locks out micronutrients (iron, zinc), giving leaves that sickly yellow look—trading one problem for another.

---

#### Quick‑fire FAQ

* **Can I cure blossom‑end rot mid‑season by dumping lime around plants?**  
  Not really. Calcium still has to move from soil → roots → fruits, and that takes time. Foliar calcium sprays can help current fruit, but the long‑term fix is steady soil Ca and even watering.

* **Eggshells instead of lime?**  
  Sure, if you grind them to dust and have the patience of a monk—coarse shells release calcium at glacial speed.

* **My soil pH is fine but I still get blossom‑end rot—why?**  
  Usually irregular watering or root damage. Calcium moves with water; drought‑flood cycles interrupt transport. Mulch and consistent moisture beat any miracle additive.

* **Is lime the same as gypsum?**  
  Gypsum is calcium sulfate. It supplies calcium **without** raising pH—useful if your pH is already in range but you still need Ca.

---

### Bottom line

1. **Test the soil**, then add lime only if pH is low or calcium is short.  
2. **Incorporate months ahead** of planting so it’s fully available.  
3. Keep **water consistent**—lime can’t compensate for drought stress.  

Give your tomatoes that spa day before planting and they’ll thank you with firm, rot‑free fruit—and you can save the *other* lime for taco night. 🌮🥳
