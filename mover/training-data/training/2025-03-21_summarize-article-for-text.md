**PERSONAL PROTECTION PLAN**  
**STAYING ALERT, AVOIDING CONFLICT, AND ACTING RESPONSIBLY | CHIP EBERHART**

Creating a personal-protection plan is critical for those living in urban communities, especially for individuals who carry concealed firearms. Urban areas pose unique challenges due to high population density and varied environments, making it essential for you to develop a strategy that prioritizes awareness and de-escalation. The primary goal is to "win the fight" through avoidance, as the best outcome in a confrontation is that it not happen in the first place. Situational awareness, conflict avoidance, and responsible firearms ownership all contribute to your personal safety and a safer community.

### RELAXED BUT ALERT  
Situational awareness is the foundation of personal protection and self-defense. It means being mindful of your surroundings, recognizing potential threats, and avoiding risky situations. Maintaining a heightened level of awareness in urban settings is crucial, as the close quarters and wide breadth of activity in these areas make it easy for you to be caught off guard.  

If you’re not situationally aware, no amount of training or expensive gear will benefit you. The Cooper Color Code, developed by Lt. Col. Jeff Cooper, categorizes situational awareness into levels ranging from “White” (unaware) to “Red” (ready to act immediately). In an urban environment, especially if you’re carrying, you should ideally stay in “Yellow” — a relaxed but alert state. This level allows for the quick perception of subtle changes in your surroundings that may indicate a potential threat.  

You may situationally aware by regularly scanning your surroundings for exits, places of cover, and unusual behavior. Noticing changes in patterns — such as shifts in crowd behavior or someone paying you close attention — can provide valuable early warning signs. Even small changes in people’s actions can alert you to a potentially unsafe situation. Make scanning your environment a game that you and your family play, similar to the childhood game of “I Spy.”  

Avoid high-risk areas. Some urban zones are statistically more prone to crime. Steer clear of isolated areas, poorly lit streets, and places with high foot traffic, especially at night. My personal motto is, “If I’m going someplace where I think I’ll need a firearm, I probably shouldn’t be going there.”

### CONFLICT AVOIDANCE  
While carrying a firearm can provide you with a sense of security, relying on it as the first line of defense can lead to dangerous consequences. The priority should always be to defuse situations rather than escalate them.  

Engaging with others in a calm, controlled manner can help de-escalate potential conflicts. Nonverbal cues, such as open hands and maintaining a non-threatening posture, can reduce the likelihood of confrontation. Knowing how to communicate assertively — rather than aggressively — is a valuable skill for you and all concealed carriers to have.  

If someone is encroaching on your space or behaving in an intimidating way, verbal boundary-setting can help. Simple statements such as "I’m not interested in trouble" and "I need you to back up" show assertiveness, but some predators may interpret them as a challenge. Instead, be more direct with a command, such as "I can hear you fine from there." This tactic works best when it’s paired with non-aggressive yet firm body language, reinforcing the message. You, not the predator, should be in control of the distance.  

The emotional aspects of an argument, especially in public space, can quickly turn dangerous. You should remember that engaging in a verbal altercation is not worth the risk, especially when you’re armed. The bigger man or woman walks away from an avoidable argument. Situational awareness can often prevent a situation from turning into conflict by recognizing early warning signs before reaching the “Boiling Point” in the October 2024 issue (Page 44).  

Visualizing potential scenarios and rehearsing responses can prepare you for conflict. Knowing what actions you would take in advance reduces hesitation and helps you make quicker, better decisions.

### RESPONSIBLY ARMED  
As a concealed carrier, you must keep in mind that responsible firearms handling and secure carrying are essential elements of personal protection. Developing good habits can prevent dangerous situations and accidents, ensuring that carrying a firearm enhances, rather than compromises, your (and others’) safety. This is the primary reason I personally prefer concealed carry over open carry. When you carry openly, you risk becoming a prime target. It’s like playing poker and putting all three of your aces on your forehead; keep your aces to yourself until your hand is called.  

You should carry your firearm in a high-quality holster that fully covers the trigger guard and provides secure retention. Likewise, walking briskly or even running may be a better option than standing around to see who’s the better marksman. A loose or poorly secured firearm (or any personal-defense tool) can quickly become your attacker’s weapon instead of yours, so investing in reliable retention for all of your gear is a must.  

Regularly practice your draw in a safe environment to avoid fumbling during an emergency. If you carry other tools, such as a defensive knife or spray, you need to practice deploying those as well. It’s better to secure defensive tools on your body with a belt or under clothing specifically designed for them. Also, consider having different tools accessible for each hand. Batman didn’t carry all of his tools on one side, and neither should you.  

It should go without saying that you should seek training from a qualified professional to enhance safety and efficacy. If you’re seeking training in an urban setting, look for courses that focus on close-quarters scenarios (e.g., training in confined spaces such as stairwells, hallways, and crowded areas), high-density environments, and defensive tactics unique to city life. Decision-making drills or training that includes stress testing, which can prepare you for split-second judgments, can be lifesavers. These should include exercises that help you identify safe versus unsafe situations and practice quickly assessing threats.  

In urban areas, laws regarding concealed carry can be particularly strict and vary greatly. Understand the rules for brandishing a weapon, as displaying a firearm without cause can result in legal repercussions. The USCCA Reciprocity Map is an excellent resource for becoming familiar with your home state’s laws as well as the laws of other states you plan to visit. Remember that "ignorance of the law is not a defense" in a responsibly armed American’s mind.

### CARRY WITH CARE  
A personal-protection plan for urban communities, particularly if you carry a firearm, hinges on situational awareness and conflict avoidance. By understanding your surroundings, practicing de-escalation techniques, and maintaining emotional control, you can significantly reduce the risk of confrontation. Firearms ownership carries serious responsibilities, and your primary objective should always be to minimize the need for its use by remaining alert, well-trained, and prepared.

**CRAFTING YOUR CASTLE**  
**ASSESSING AND ENHANCING YOUR HOME’S SECURITY | MICHAEL JANICH**  

We’ve all heard the saying, “A man’s home is his castle.” Paraphrased from a 1604 legal decision by Sir Edward Coke, attorney general of England, the original quote was, “That the house of every one is to him as his Castle and Fortress as well for defence against injury and violence, as for his repose.”  

Applied to modern self-defense law, this forms the foundation of the concept known as "Castle Doctrine." In principle, it makes perfect, if not eloquent, sense. Putting it into practice, however, requires a bit more work.  

The word "castle" conjures up visions of a structure that is virtually impenetrable, offering excellent physical protection to its occupants and contents. If breached, its design also allows you to effectively defend it. With these goals in mind, let’s take a step-by-step look at assessing and enhancing your home’s security.  

### FROM THE OUTSIDE LOOKING IN  
The first step in evaluating your home’s security should be to step outside and assess it through the eyes of a potential burglar or home invader. Ideally, you should do it together with family members or friends stationed both inside and outside the house. The goal is to identify points where someone could avoid being seen from the inside or outside — or both. Blind spots near entry points, such as doors and windows, are of particular concern, as they can provide criminals with both time and concealment to break in. These areas can also function as points for an ambush when you enter or exit your home or open the door for a visitor.  

You can mitigate some blind spots through simple measures, such as trimming bushes. Others can be made visible by installing strategically placed parabolic mirrors or, even better, security cameras.  

Homes without security systems are far more likely to be burglarized than those equipped with them. A study from UNC Charlotte found that 83 percent of burglars actively look for security cameras before attempting a break-in, and 60 percent would choose a different target if they saw a security system. Clearly, investing in a security system— and advertising its presence with visible signs — is an effective way to protect your home. Though some experts suggest that posting alarm-system signs alone is good enough, criminals are often wise to this trick. If you can afford it, invest in the real thing.  

One low-cost alternative (or supplement) to security cameras is motion-activated security lights. Even if you can’t see an area from inside your home, these lights can alert you to activity and deny a potential invader the cover of darkness.  

Finally, while you’re outside conducting your assessment, look for anything that might give a potential intruder an advantage, such as ladders, tools, or other objects that could aid in gaining entry.  

### HARDENING YOUR HOME  
According to ADT, these are the most common points of entry for home break-ins:  
- **Front doors (34 percent)**  
- **First-floor windows (23 percent)**  
- **Back doors (22 percent)**  
- **Garage doors (9 percent)**  
- **Basements (4 percent)**  

FBI data indicates that about 56 percent of burglaries involve forcible entry. Surprisingly, most burglaries occur during daylight hours.  

One of the most effective steps you can take to protect your home is to harden its physical security. Take a hard look at your front door and any other hinged exterior doors in your home. Each should be solid-core or steel-sheathed and free of windows that, if broken, would allow someone to reach in and unlock the door. A proper door should also avoid a pet door or mail slot, as savvy criminals can exploit either of these to unlock the door from the inside. A peephole, however, is a valuable addition provided it’s positioned so that it is accessible to everyone in your family.  

Next, check each door’s hinges. They should be on the inside, allowing the door to open inward and preventing hinge pins from being removed from the outside. Use a screwdriver to remove one of the hinge screws. Chances are it will be about .75 inches long, just enough to anchor into the door jamb. If that’s the case, replace all of your hinge screws with extra-long screws, such as 3-inch deck screws, that pass through the jamb and anchor into the structural framing behind it.  

On the lock side, don’t settle for an ordinary doorknob lock. This is inadequate and can be easily defeated. Instead, install a high-quality, name-brand deadbolt lock with a 1-inch bolt throw. If your door is not steel-sheathed, strengthen the area around the lock with a metal door reinforcement. This supports the stile at its weakest point: where it’s drilled to accommodate the lock hardware.  

For a deadbolt lock to be effective, it must anchor securely to the door frame. The small, two-screw strike plate included with most locks isn’t sufficient for this. Replace it with an extended, high-security strike plate, typically about 8 to 10 inches long and with multiple mounting holes. Use extra-long screws to anchor the plate securely into the framing structure behind the door frame.  

### PATIO DOORS AND WINDOWS  
If your home has a sliding patio door, don’t rely only on the latch mechanism as a lock. Criminals often use suction-style handles (similar to shower safety grips) to unlock such a door and release the latch. While the common trick of placing a piece of broomstick in the door track may work, criminals are often wise to that too. A better solution is a purpose-designed security bar that wedges between the door and the frame above floor level. You can also mount aftermarket bolts to lock your sliding door securely to the frame.  

Take steps to secure all of your ground-level windows and any easily accessible second-story windows. A sliding window should be equipped with one or more secure locks. To supplement these, carefully cut a .75-inch or 1-inch hardwood dowel to wedge between the closed window and the opposite edge of the frame. Drill a hole through one end of the dowel and install a short loop of parachute cord. This makeshift shield provides you with a solid purchase to pull the dowel free and makes each dowel a handy impact weapon should an intruder gain entry.  

### SECURITY GATES  
One inherent weakness in many modern home designs is a window located immediately adjacent to the front door. This severely weakens the frame structure and compromises the door’s function as a barrier. A home invader can easily break the window and reach through to unlock the door. Alternatively, an intruder can simply kick in the door, as the jamb next to the window is weak and provides minimal structural support.  

One excellent solution to this flaw is a security gate that spans both the door and the adjacent window, mounted securely to the exterior of the house. Made of sturdy steel and equipped with a double-keyed deadbolt lock, it not only sends a strong deterrent message to potential intruders but also doubles as a screen door. In addition to allowing fresh air inside, it enables you to greet visitors face-to-face while maintaining the protection of a strong physical barrier.  

### USE WHAT YOU’VE GOT  
The best physical barriers in the world won’t help if you don’t actually use them. To keep your home truly secure, take full use of these resources by developing good habits. You also need to instill these habits in your family members until they become second nature.  

Most of the context of this article has focused on physical security measures to prevent home break-ins. A Bureau of Justice Statistics report on victimization during household burglaries found that most burglaries (72.4 percent) occurred when residents were not home. However, in 27.6 percent of cases, residents were home. And that’s when violent victimization is most likely to occur.  

Your home can be your castle, but only if you and your family are diligent enough to consistently raise the drawbridge and fill the moat.

**"YOU MUST CARRY A FIREARM. THERE IS NO OTHER WAY TO LEVEL THE PLAYING FIELD. THE LIKELIHOOD OF YOU FACING SOMEONE YOUNGER, STRONGER AND MEANER IS HIGH."**  

The Greek philosopher Heraclitus taught that change is the only constant in life. In my experience, I can attest that he was absolutely correct. As we age, we pay the price of getting older—becoming less agile, less flexible, and less capable. Our self-defense needs also evolve as we face infirmity or disability, and our need for protection grows as we become weaker. Any visible infirmity can mark us as potential targets.  

So, how can you make yourself less attractive to criminals and reduce the risk of victimization? And how do you prepare for potential attacks? Following these tips is a good start:  

### **MIND OVER MATTER**  
If you are to survive, the first thing you must do is be honest with yourself. In facing a catastrophic illness, I have realized that the best approach is to admit that I’m not as capable as I once was. My energy level and my balance have been affected by my condition, and I’ve had to cultivate a more positive outlook on life. It’s essential to have a source of faith and to understand that you have survived for a reason. Embracing the belief that you are strong enough to endure but mentally prepared to protect yourself and those around you.  

### **PRACTICING SITUATIONAL AWARENESS**  
When physical ability is limited, cultivating a high level of situational awareness becomes vital. In your condition, you cannot afford to be behind the curve. You must be acutely aware of your surroundings and of anyone passing through your zone who could pose a potential threat. This awareness must be practiced without bias or preconceived notions, as they can cloud your judgment.  

A wise man once told me that you never truly know who a killer is until he or she is killing you. Just look at the news, and you will see men, women, and, in some cases, children who have committed terrible crimes. Evil is not limited and can come in many forms.  

Stay informed by following the news. Be aware of the most prevalent crimes in your community and prepare accordingly. Gathering intelligence provides insight and, in some instances, seeing the faces of those who commit such crimes helps you commit those faces to memory.  

### **INCORPORATING OBSERVATIONAL PSYCHOLOGY**  
A key component of developing heightened situational awareness is understanding observational psychology. You must learn to see and listen with intent. Your first line of defense is gathering intelligence from your surroundings and quickly interpreting it. This buys you time, which is absolutely necessary for survival.  

My father, a combat vet, taught me a valuable lesson when he said, “The greatest warrior in the world is useless if he is unable to perceive the warrior in the dark before the war.” I was just a kid when he told me that, and it took me years before I understood what he was trying to tell me.  

I’ve been a martial artist and weapons enthusiast all of my life. I’ve had some of the best training money can buy. But if I misperceive a valid threat, it will all be buried with me. Learning to read body language and facial expressions—along with interpreting the sounds that precede violence—is how you can avoid or anticipate an attack.  

### **SELECTING YOUR EQUIPMENT**  
The choices you make regarding your personal protection devices are critical if you have diminished health. First, you must carry a firearm. There is no other way to level the playing field. The likelihood of you facing someone younger, stronger, and meaner is high. Above all, your skill set must match your current capabilities.  

However, your physical condition may require that you select a firearm with less recoil. A .22 is not ideal for self-defense, but neither is shaking a firearm and using it as a steel knuckle. I know several people who have successfully stopped attackers with .22s and .25s. Stress the use of firearms because, in your weakened state, you don’t want to find yourself struggling to fight off an attacker with your hands alone.  

Another consideration is the type of firearm you should choose. Reduced strength may make a revolver a more attractive option for you, but recoil and the double-action trigger pull may be a lot to manage. I also recommend looking at Smith & Wesson’s EZ models and Ruger’s Lite Rack. I consider these viable choices for those with limited grip strength or wrist strength.  

How will you carry it? If you use a wheelchair or crutches, appendix carry or ankle carry may not be a practical option. I don’t prefer off-body carry, but I’ve learned that, at times, it can be a practical choice for me.  

Other types of weapons you already carry—for instance, a knife and pepper spray—can be lifesavers and get within your attacker’s reach, buying you time and distance to access your firearm.  

### **DON’T UNDERESTIMATE TRAINING**  
You must determine your defensive strategy and build your training around it. Consider taking a class on observational psychology along with a firearms class. Spend time accessing and practicing with your weapons. Incorporate stress into your training, as it can significantly affect how smoothly you draw your firearm. Make time to go to the gym to strengthen your body. Work to recover what you have lost or at least regain as much strength as possible. Acknowledge and accept the fact that this process is going to be uncomfortable and, at times, painful.  

Spend time training in the martial arts. With the growing popularity of mixed martial arts competitions, jujitsu and grappling are more accessible than ever. If you are ill or disabled, any attack is likely to take you to the ground. Limited stamina and mobility make it important to learn how to defend yourself from that position. I always suggest this because this training has been beneficial in my own rehabilitation. If it worked for me, it could work for you too.  

### **PREPARE, PERCEIVE AND PROTECT**  
If you are disabled or ill, your ability to defend yourself doesn’t disappear. You are not helpless; you can still protect yourself, your family, and your friends.  

It starts by being smarter than the wolves. Build an effective strategy and practice it. Work on strengthening yourself. Seek out as much training as possible and spend as much time as you can at the range. If you are honest with yourself and prepare accordingly, you can stay safe. Repeat after me: **Prepare, perceive, and protect.** Commit these three Ps to memory and practice them daily.
